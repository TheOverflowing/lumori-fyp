## 题目

The following passages are fictional teaching texts written for this exercise. Read them as two first-person accounts of the same community archive exhibition, then answer the question below.

Passage A — the curator of the imagined North Quay community archive writes: We finally opened the exhibition together. Volunteers carried boxes from their homes, and children stopped before the wall of photographs. We chose captions short enough to read in a crowded room. The opening felt like a beginning, not a finished history. Passage A does not identify who selected the photographs, list rejected contributions, or report every visitor's reaction.

Passage B — a volunteer from the same imagined exhibition writes: I recognized my father's coat in a photograph, though the caption named only the factory. I was glad that someone had kept the picture. When the curator thanked us, I applauded, but I kept the letter I had brought inside my bag. Perhaps there would be another occasion to show it. Passage B reports gratitude and hesitation but does not state why the letter was withheld, whether anybody rejected it, or what it contained.

Interpretive notes for this exercise:
1. A first-person account gives access to a situated viewpoint, not automatic knowledge of every participant's motives.
2. Distinguish a narrator's explicit statement from an inference based on an image, an omission or a contrast.
3. The curator's collective we can be read as an aspiration to shared ownership or as a phrase that leaves differences within the group unstated.
4. The volunteer's applause and unopened letter can support readings of tentative belonging, unresolved exclusion, or a mixture, but the text does not establish censorship or identify the letter's contents.
5. A defensible interpretation compares details from both passages, acknowledges a plausible competing reading, and explains the limits of its claim; different interpretations can meet these standards without agreeing on a single hidden intention.

Guidelines for your answer:
- Do not assume outside history, biography or literary theory; use only the details in Passages A and B and notes 1–5.
- Multiple readings are acceptable if they are supported by specific details and are honest about what the passages do not say.

Using only these passages and notes, write a short essay (roughly 200–300 words) that:
(a) cites specific details from both accounts as the textual basis for a reading of the exhibition's shared ownership;
(b) compares how the two narrators are positioned and where their accounts of the same exhibition diverge or align;
(c) explains how the curator's collective we can be read either as an aspiration to shared ownership or as leaving differences unstated;
(d) offers a defensible reading of the volunteer's applause alongside the withheld letter, and names at least one plausible competing reading;
(e) states at least one thing the passages do not establish; and
(f) closes with an explicit judgment about shared ownership, justified by your comparisons rather than a single presumed hidden intention.

Quote or name the details you rely on, and keep every claim within what the two passages and notes allow.

## 参考答案

A defensible response could be built as follows, though other grounded readings are acceptable.

(a) Textual basis. The curator's account is voiced through a collective "we" that carries boxes, chooses short captions and opens the exhibition "together," so the account presents ownership as distributed among volunteers, children and the curator. The volunteer's account anchors the same exhibition in one body and one object: a father's coat recognized in a photograph whose caption named only the factory, plus an applauded thank-you and a letter kept inside a bag.

(b) Positions and divergence. The curator speaks from a position of responsibility for how the exhibition is framed ("We finally opened," "We chose captions"), which fits an account of shared ownership. The volunteer speaks from a position of partial recognition: the coat is visible, the person behind it is not named, and something the volunteer brought stays private. The two accounts align on the fact of a completed, crowded, valued opening; they diverge on how fully any one participant is included in what was shown.

(c) The collective "we." As note 3 says, "we" can read as an aspiration to shared ownership — the exhibition belongs to those who carried boxes and chose captions. The same phrasing can read as smoothing over differences, since the curator's "we chose" does not say who chose, and Passage A does not identify who selected photographs or what was left out.

(d) Applause and the withheld letter. The volunteer applauds the curator's thanks yet keeps the letter in the bag, which supports tentative belonging: participation is real, but full display of a personal claim on the archive is deferred. A plausible competing reading is unresolved exclusion — the caption naming only the factory leaves the father unnamed, so the deferred letter marks a gap the exhibition did not close. The passages do not settle which reading is correct, and note 4 says both are available.

(e) Limits. The passages do not establish censorship, do not state why the letter was withheld, do not reveal its contents, and do not identify who selected the photographs or list rejected contributions.

(f) Judgment. Shared ownership is best described as real but incomplete and unevenly felt: the exhibition was genuinely opened and made together, as Passage A shows, yet Passage B shows one contributor's attachment to it remaining partly unshown. That judgment rests on comparing the collective "we" with the volunteer's coat, applause and bagged letter, not on assuming a hidden intention behind either account.

## 解析

The task rewards a reading that uses both accounts and the given notes rather than one passage alone.

Strong answers will:
- ground the argument in specific details (collective "we," short captions, crowded room; father's coat, factory caption, applause, letter in the bag);
- compare the two narrators' positions and say where their accounts agree and diverge;
- give both readings of the collective "we" as note 3 allows;
- interpret the applause with the withheld letter and name a plausible competing reading, as note 4 permits;
- state limits (no censorship shown, no reason for withholding, no letter contents, no record of who selected photographs);
- close with an explicit judgment about shared ownership supported by those comparisons.

A response that treats the letter as proof of censorship, invents the letter's contents or the photographer's identity, or appeals to outside history does not meet the standard. A response that argues shared ownership is largely achieved, or largely incomplete, can both count if it cites the passages and acknowledges the competing reading.
