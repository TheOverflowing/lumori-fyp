## 题目

Read the two fictional accounts of the same imagined exhibition and the accompanying teaching note, then write a short interpretive response.

Curator's account (fiction_archive_curator_en):
"We finally opened the exhibition together. Volunteers carried boxes from their homes, and children stopped before the wall of photographs. We chose captions short enough to read in a crowded room. The opening felt like a beginning, not a finished history." The narrator is responsible for the exhibition but "does not identify who selected the photographs, list rejected contributions, or report every visitor's reaction."

Volunteer's account (fiction_archive_volunteer_en):
"I recognized my father's coat in a photograph, though the caption named only the factory. I was glad that someone had kept the picture. When the curator thanked us, I applauded, but I kept the letter I had brought inside my bag. Perhaps there would be another occasion to show it." The narrator "reports gratitude and hesitation but does not state why the letter was withheld, whether anybody rejected it, or what it contained."

Teaching note (fiction_archive_method_en):
"A first-person account gives access to a situated viewpoint, not automatic knowledge of every participant's motives." It advises distinguishing "a narrator's explicit statement from an inference based on an image, omission or contrast," notes that "the curator's collective we can be read as an aspiration to shared ownership or as a phrase that leaves differences within the group unstated," and states that the volunteer's "applause and unopened letter can support readings of tentative belonging, unresolved exclusion, or a mixture, but the text does not establish censorship or identify the letter's contents."

In your response, address all of the following:
1. Cite one specific detail from the curator's account that presents the opening as shared or collective, and one specific detail from the volunteer's account that qualifies that inclusion.
2. Contrast how each narrator's position shapes what that narrator can and cannot report about the exhibition.
3. Offer one defensible reading of the volunteer's applause alongside the kept letter, using specific details.
4. Explain one contrasting reading of the same details that would also be coherent, and say what evidence would be needed to decide between them.
5. State at least one interpretive limit the passages impose.

Your answer is judged on textual support, attention to counterevidence, and staying within what the passages establish; more than one reading is acceptable.

## 参考答案

A defensible response could run as follows.

1. Shared/collective detail (curator): "We finally opened the exhibition together" and "We chose captions short enough to read in a crowded room" present the opening as a joint effort by a collective we. Qualifying detail (volunteer): although the volunteer says "I applauded" when thanked, "I kept the letter I had brought inside my bag," so participation is accompanied by a withholding that the collective account does not register.

2. Position shapes reporting. As the person responsible, the curator speaks in a collective we and frames the event as "a beginning, not a finished history," which suits an account of shared ownership but, by the passage's own admission, leaves out who selected the photographs, which contributions were rejected, and many visitor reactions. The volunteer speaks from a single situated viewpoint and can report a personal detail (recognizing "my father's coat" beneath a caption that "named only the factory") and an inner hesitation (keeping the letter), but cannot report others' motives or whether the letter was ever rejected.

3. Defensible reading: the applause and the kept letter together support reading the volunteer's belonging as tentative. The volunteer is “glad that someone had kept the picture” and applauds the curator, yet keeps the letter, suggesting partial rather than full inclusion. The note allows this as one of several readings.

4. Coherent contrasting reading: the same details could show unresolved exclusion, or a mixture of belonging and exclusion, since the caption names only the factory and the letter stays in the bag. Alternatively, the kept letter could show not exclusion but a simple wait for another occasion, as the volunteer says “Perhaps there would be another occasion to show it.” To decide between the readings, evidence would be needed about why the letter was withheld, whether anyone rejected or discouraged it, and what it contained — none of which the passages provide.

5. Interpretive limits: the passages do not establish censorship, do not identify the letter's contents, and do not reveal every participant's motives or which photographs were selected or rejected. The teaching note states that a first-person account gives "a situated viewpoint, not automatic knowledge of every participant's motives," so claims about hidden intentions, selection policy, or the letter's content exceed the evidence.

## 解析 / 评分说明

Which claims are explicitly stated:
The curator says the opening was done “together” and that “we chose captions.” The volunteer says “I applauded” and “I kept the letter I had brought inside my bag.”

Which claims require inference:
Reading the kept letter as exclusion, or as patient waiting, is an inference from omission and contrast, not an explicit statement. The teaching note warns to separate the narrator's explicit statement from an inference based on an image, omission or contrast.

Why multiple answers are acceptable:
The note says the applause and letter “can support readings of tentative belonging, unresolved exclusion, or a mixture,” and that different interpretations can meet the standards without identical wording or agreement on a single hidden intention. A response earns credit by citing passages, acknowledging a competing reading, and naming the evidence that would settle the question.

What the passages do not establish:
They do not show censorship, do not identify the letter's contents, and do not report all visitor reactions or which photographs were selected or rejected. Any response asserting those facts goes beyond the evidence.
