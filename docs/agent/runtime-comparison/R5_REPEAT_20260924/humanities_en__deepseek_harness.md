## 题目

Read the three supplied fictional passages, then write a short interpretive essay responding to the prompt at the end.

Passage 1 — Curator's account (fiction_archive_curator_en): The curator of the imagined North Quay community archive writes: "We finally opened the exhibition together. Volunteers carried boxes from their homes, and children stopped before the wall of photographs. We chose captions short enough to read in a crowded room. The opening felt like a beginning, not a finished history." The passage does not identify who selected the photographs, list rejected contributions, or report every visitor's reaction.

Passage 2 — Volunteer's account (fiction_archive_volunteer_en): A volunteer from the same imagined exhibition writes: "I recognized my father's coat in a photograph, though the caption named only the factory. I was glad that someone had kept the picture. When the curator thanked us, I applauded, but I kept the letter I had brought inside my bag. Perhaps there would be another occasion to show it." The passage reports gratitude and hesitation but does not state why the letter was withheld, whether anybody rejected it, or what it contained.

Passage 3 — Teaching note on perspective (fiction_archive_method_en): A first-person account gives access to a situated viewpoint, not automatic knowledge of every participant's motives. Distinguish a narrator's explicit statement from an inference based on an image, omission or contrast. The curator's collective "we" can be read as an aspiration to shared ownership or as a phrase that leaves differences within the group unstated. The volunteer's applause and unopened letter can support readings of tentative belonging, unresolved exclusion, or a mixture, but the text does not establish censorship or identify the letter's contents. A defensible interpretation compares details from both passages, acknowledges a plausible competing reading, and explains the limits of its claim.

In your response, complete all of the following:
1. Explain, in your own words, why a first-person account gives a situated viewpoint rather than automatic access to other participants' motives.
2. Identify at least three details the curator's account states explicitly, and at least two things it does not report.
3. Identify at least three details the volunteer's account states explicitly, and mark clearly which of your claims about those details are inference rather than explicit statement.
4. Contrast how the two narrators present the exhibition's shared ownership: point to one place where the accounts converge and one where they diverge.
5. Offer one defensible reading of the volunteer's applause and retained letter, citing specific wording, without claiming the text establishes censorship or reveals the letter's contents.
6. Develop a plausible competing reading of the same details, say which reading the given evidence better supports and why, and state the limits of your interpretive claim.

Write a continuous short essay (roughly 300–450 words). Do not use outside historical or literary theory; stay within the three passages.

## 参考答案

Any response that meets all six requirements is acceptable; wording and final verdict may differ. A model response:

1. A first-person account reports what one participant saw, felt and chose to tell, so it can describe the curator's decision to keep captions short or the volunteer's decision to keep the letter in the bag, but it cannot enter another person's mind. The curator says the opening "felt like a beginning, not a finished history," which is the curator's own sense of the event, not evidence of how the volunteer or the children felt.

2. The curator's account explicitly states: the exhibition was opened "together"; volunteers carried boxes "from their homes"; children stopped before the wall of photographs; captions were chosen "short enough to read in a crowded room"; and the opening felt "like a beginning, not a finished history." It does not report who selected the photographs, whether any contributions were rejected, or how visitors beyond the children reacted.

3. The volunteer explicitly states: the coat in a photograph was the father's; the caption named only the factory; the volunteer was "glad that someone had kept the picture"; the volunteer applauded when the curator thanked the volunteers; and the letter stayed in the bag, with the thought that "perhaps there would be another occasion." That the applause and the withheld letter express unresolved exclusion, or alternatively tentative belonging, is inference; the text reports the gesture and the withholding but not their cause.

4. Convergence: both accounts present the exhibition as a shared effort — the curator's "we ... opened ... together" and the volunteers carrying boxes meet the volunteer's participation and applause. Divergence: the curator's collective "we" presents the opening as unified, whereas the volunteer's account shows a participant who applauds yet keeps something back; the first passage does not mention the letter or any hesitation, and the second does not describe the exhibition as a whole or speak for other visitors.

5. One defensible reading: the applause and the letter kept in the bag together suggest partial, cautious belonging. The volunteer is glad the photograph was kept and joins the thanks, yet the letter stays out of the exhibition, and "perhaps there would be another occasion" keeps the question open rather than closing it. The text does not say the letter was refused, so this is not censorship, and the passage never states the letter's contents, so no claim about them is warranted.

6. Competing reading: the same details may be read as unresolved exclusion — the caption naming only the factory leaves the father's identity unrecorded, and the retained letter can suggest that the volunteer does not feel fully included. Which reading is better supported depends on where one places weight: the explicit "I was glad" and the applause support belonging, while the caption's silence and the withheld letter support exclusion. Neither passage settles the question, so the strongest claim is the modest one: the volunteer's position is mixed or unresolved, and any stronger claim (for example, that the archive suppressed the letter) exceeds the evidence. Limits: we have only two situated viewpoints, the curator's account is silent on the letter, and the volunteer's account does not explain the withholding — so motive and intent cannot be established, only the gestures and their openness to more than one reading.

## 解析

The task rewards a bounded reading that separates what the passages state from what a reader infers, and that tests a preferred interpretation against a plausible rival. Strong responses name explicit details from each passage, use the collective "we" and the withheld letter to show convergence and divergence on shared ownership, and refuse claims the text does not support, such as censorship or the letter's contents.

Weaker responses treat the volunteer's hesitation as proof of exclusion (or the applause as proof of belonging) without weighing the other passage, merge the two narrators' viewpoints into one voice, or assert details about the letter that no passage gives.

Wording and final verdict are open; what matters is that each requirement is addressed with cited details and a stated limit.
