## 题目

Scenario: In an introductory humanities seminar, you are comparing two fictional first-person accounts of the same imagined community archive exhibition, plus a supplied note on narrative perspective. All people, places, and events are fictional AI-authored teaching material. Use only the three supplied excerpts.

Passage A - Curator's account (fictional):
The curator of the imagined North Quay community archive writes: We finally opened the exhibition together. Volunteers carried boxes from their homes, and children stopped before the wall of photographs. We chose captions short enough to read in a crowded room. The opening felt like a beginning, not a finished history. The passage is narrated by someone responsible for the exhibition. It does not identify who selected the photographs, list rejected contributions, or report every visitor's reaction.

Passage B - Volunteer's account (fictional):
A volunteer from the same imagined exhibition writes: I recognized my father's coat in a photograph, though the caption named only the factory. I was glad that someone had kept the picture. When the curator thanked us, I applauded, but I kept the letter I had brought inside my bag. Perhaps there would be another occasion to show it. The narrator reports gratitude and hesitation but does not state why the letter was withheld, whether anybody rejected it, or what it contained.

Note - Perspective and interpretive limits (fictional teaching note):
A first-person account gives access to a situated viewpoint, not automatic knowledge of every participant's motives. Distinguish a narrator's explicit statement from an inference based on an image, omission or contrast. The curator's collective we can be read as an aspiration to shared ownership or as a phrase that leaves differences within the group unstated. The volunteer's applause and unopened letter can support readings of tentative belonging, unresolved exclusion, or a mixture, but the text does not establish censorship or identify the letter's contents. A defensible interpretation compares details from both passages, acknowledges a plausible competing reading, and explains the limits of its claim. Different interpretations can meet these standards without using identical wording or agreeing on a single hidden intention.

Two classmates offer competing claims:
Claim 1: The curator's 'we' and the volunteer's applause show that the exhibition created shared ownership that everyone experienced as belonging.
Claim 2: The volunteer's unopened letter proves the exhibition censored or excluded her family history.

Evaluate both claims and construct your own interpretation under these constraints:
1. Distinguish what each passage explicitly states from inferences about motives, selection, rejection, or withheld information.
2. Use at least one concrete detail from each account as textual support.
3. Compare how the curator's collective language and the volunteer's applause coexist with hesitation or unstated difference.
4. Address a plausible competing reading drawn from the same passages.
5. Limit claims to what the passages establish; do not assert censorship, the letter's contents, or unstated visitor reactions.
6. Explain how a specific omission or ambiguity shapes your interpretation and state the uncertainty that remains.

Then:
- Decide which claim, if either, better fits the evidence and the constraints, and justify your judgment.
- If neither claim is fully defensible, revise one claim into a qualified sentence that could satisfy the constraints.
- Explain what additional evidence would be needed to support the stronger original claim you reject.

Do not invent details about the letter, the selection process, or visitors' reactions.

## 参考答案

Source facts:
- Passage A states that the curator and volunteers opened the exhibition together, volunteers carried boxes, children stopped before photographs, captions were chosen to be short, and the opening felt like a beginning rather than a finished history.
- Passage A does not identify who selected photographs, list rejected contributions, or report every visitor's reaction.
- Passage B states that the volunteer recognized her father's coat in a photograph, was glad someone kept the picture, applauded when the curator thanked the group, and kept the letter she had brought inside her bag, thinking perhaps there would be another occasion to show it.
- Passage B does not state why the letter was withheld, whether anybody rejected it, or what it contained.
- The note states that first-person accounts give situated viewpoints, not automatic knowledge of every participant's motives, and that a defensible interpretation compares both passages, acknowledges a competing reading, and explains its limits.

Evaluation of the claims:
- Claim 1 is only partly supportable. The curator's collective 'we' and the volunteer's applause are textual details that can support a reading of shared occasion and some belonging. But the claim that 'everyone experienced as belonging' goes beyond the passages: Passage A does not report every visitor's reaction, and Passage B records hesitation alongside applause because the volunteer keeps the letter in her bag. The 'we' may be aspirational or may leave differences unstated, as the note warns.
- Claim 2 is not defensible as stated. The volunteer keeping the letter in her bag shows hesitation or an unshared family history, but the passages do not say that anyone rejected the letter, that the exhibition censored it, or what the letter contained. The note explicitly says the text does not establish censorship or identify the letter's contents. Reading the unopened letter as proof of exclusion converts an omission into an unsupported motive.

Qualified interpretation (one defensible response):
The exhibition can be read as a shared occasion whose meaning was not fully shared. The curator's 'we' and the short captions support a collective, public-facing account of opening the archive together. The volunteer's applause supports gratitude and participation. At the same time, the letter kept in her bag supports a reading of tentative belonging or unresolved difference: she had something family-related that she did not show. The passages do not settle whether this was personal hesitation, a sense of exclusion, or simply a decision to wait for another occasion.

Competing reading:
A reader could emphasize the positive side more strongly: the volunteer was glad the photograph was kept, she applauded, and she imagines 'another occasion,' which may signal hope rather than exclusion. That reading is plausible but still cannot support 'everyone experienced belonging' or 'censorship.'

Uncertainty and omission:
The key omission is why the letter stayed in the bag. Because the text does not say it was rejected or even submitted, the omission limits what can be claimed about exclusion. It shapes the interpretation by making the exhibition's inclusiveness unresolved rather than proven or disproven.

Revised claim:
A qualified revision of Claim 1 could be: 'The curator's collective we and the volunteer's applause support a reading of shared occasion and some sense of participation, but the volunteer's decision to keep the letter in her bag leaves the completeness of that belonging uncertain.'
A qualified revision of Claim 2 could be: 'The volunteer's unopened letter suggests an unshared family history or hesitation, but the passages do not establish rejection or censorship.'

Additional evidence needed for the stronger rejected claims:
- For Claim 1's 'everyone': statements from other visitors or volunteers about their experience, or a narrator's report of broad reactions.
- For Claim 2's 'censored or excluded': evidence that the letter was submitted and rejected, a stated selection rule that excluded such objects, or a participant saying the family history was deliberately kept out.
Such evidence would need to come from the supplied text; it is not present here.

Other qualified interpretations may satisfy the constraints if they cite details from both accounts, acknowledge a competing reading, and avoid asserting censorship, hidden motives, or unstated reactions.
